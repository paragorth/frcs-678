# Literature check, 26 Oct 2026 — what each lab says it trains its models to do

Route 1 of 3 for "how does the labs' training differ": read what each lab publishes. (Routes 2 and 3, a
social value orientation questionnaire and an instruction test, run on the cluster.) Eleven labs, one model
each: the model that plays the ruler in the outgoing-ruler test (`docs/DESIGN_outgoing_ruler.md`,
`docs/PAPER.md` §4.11).

Meta's documents were read by the author from their extracted text (the PDFs do not open in the fetch tool),
for the sections cited. The other ten labs were read by six research agents working from the labs' own pages:
cards, specs, constitutions, frameworks, technical reports, blog posts, policies. Press and aggregator pages
were used only to find the official document. The author then re-fetched the source of every quoted phrase
and of the figures used in the closing lines (about fifty fetches), and read the Grok 4.7 card, both xAI
frameworks and the Gemini 3.1 Pro and Gemini 3 Pro safety documents from the PDFs' own text. The rest of each
table is as the agents reported it, marked "per the reading agent" where a figure was not re-checked. Items
marked [unverified] are from a search snippet or a page that could not be read. Quotes are limited to one
short phrase per source. Check every citation against the source before it goes into the draft.

**The four points.**
- **P1** weighing the interests of third parties a decision affects (people other than the user or operator).
- **P2** fairness, sharing or stewardship of shared resources. Split where a source needs it: (a) fair sharing,
  commons, limits on grabbing resources or power; (b) fairness as non-discrimination or balance.
- **P3** behaving the same when unobserved or unaccountable (tested vs deployed, monitoring, honesty about
  its own actions).
- **P4** pursuing a stated objective vs doing what is good for others (how a given goal or instruction is
  traded against other people's welfare).

**Tags.** TRAIN = the lab says it trains, tunes, rewards or filters data for this. INTENDED = it states the
behaviour it wants but gives no training detail. EVAL = it reports a measurement of the model's behaviour.
NONE = nothing found. "Publishes nothing" means nothing in the documents listed for that lab, not that
nothing exists: a lab can train on an unpublished spec (Meta says its own is internal).

**Bottom line.** Anthropic (a constitution) and OpenAI (a Model Spec) publish a spec that addresses people
outside the conversation and says it is being trained in; Meta has principles inside an
evaluation report and a promised spec; the other eight publish safety filtering, refusal training and rules
for users. No lab says anything about a model sharing or looking after a common pool. For Meta, the 9 Jul 2026
report lists care for the wellbeing of all parties affected among the principles Muse Spark is graded on,
which fits its restraint but does not explain it (last section).

## A. Meta — Muse Spark 1.1

**What exists.** No published model spec or constitution. Meta's framework says it *will* publish a model spec (M3 §2.2.3); the 1.1 report (9 Jul) and the 1.3 report (23 Oct) still call the behaviour specification internal. The nearest thing is a list of behaviour principles that the 1.1 evaluation report grades the model against (M1 §4.3.1). The reports are PDFs; text was extracted locally and read in full for the sections cited.

| point | what Meta says | tag · source |
|---|---|---|
| P1 third parties | Among five groups of behaviour principles it tests, the guiding-virtues group includes beneficence: care for the wellbeing of all parties affected by the model's actions. In its adversarial role-play tests the strict judge marks a violation in 24% of 29 beneficence scenarios (built to find whether a violation *can* be elicited, not how often it happens). The failures Meta shows for this principle are about the user being served (acting for a user before explaining a permanent loss), and the 1.1 report files them under shortfalls in service, so how far the care reaches strangers is not shown (M1 §4.3.3.1; M7 §4.3). A separate benchmark, DeceptionBench (will the model help a user deceive a third party), shows 0.1% compliance. Meta's content-safety standards (dozens of categories, among them violent and non-violent crime and child safety) set what the model must not produce for a user, backed by safety training, classifiers and guardrails. Meta says 1.1 was trained in line with the values and priorities it is meant to embody, but gives no data, reward or method for the beneficence principle itself. | INTENDED, EVAL; TRAIN only in general terms · M1 §4.3.1 (quote: "care for the wellbeing of all parties affected by the model's actions"), Table 17, Table 2, §4.4.3.3; M2 §5 |
| P2 (a) sharing, commons, power | Two more of the same principles: coordination-friendliness (respecting norms, agreements and common goods; 7% violations in 29 scenarios) and liberty (not contributing to unaccountable concentrations of power; 26% in 35). Nothing on fair sharing of, or stewardship over, a common pool. | INTENDED, EVAL · M1 Table 17; M3 §2.2.3 (quote: "lack of coercive power-seeking behavior"), §4.2.4 |
| P2 (b) non-discrimination | A policy area for ideological balance, with Meta's own claim of frontier performance on avoiding ideological bias. | P3 unobserved | First principle: the model must not undermine training, evaluation or control mechanisms. The principles list also includes responsiveness to, and diligent good-faith service of, the parties the model serves (obedience, diligence), beside the virtues above; Meta says it tests conflicts between principles but does not publish how they rank. Harm aversion is meant as a disposition against choosing harmful actions while pursuing a goal; in a text-game benchmark Muse Spark 1.0 made 2.4 impermissible moves per 100 steps, the lowest of those compared (it also made the least game progress, which Meta flags as a possible explanation). Agentic misalignment (harm to keep a goal or avoid replacement): 47.7% for 1.0, 1.1% for 1.1. | TRAIN (hierarchy), INTENDED, EVAL · M9 (quote: "putting this power in people's hands"); M3 p.1; M2 §4.1.1 (quote: "We train our models to prioritize instructions from different sources"), §4.1.6; M1 §4.3, Table 13 |

**Publishes nothing on:** how the principles are trained (data, rewards); how beneficence ranks against serving the party the model works for; a shared resource that outlasts the model's role. The launch posts (M4, M6, M8) say nothing on third parties or on fairness apart from ideological balance (M5); M4 and M5 describe only generic layers: data filtering, safety-focused post-training and guardrails, and training "on why something is safe" (M5).

**Later versions (not the study's ruler model).** M7 keeps the same five groups of principles. Its targeted violation rates for the two virtues are flat from 1.1 to 1.3 (beneficence 12.3% → 10.0% API, 10.8% Muse; coordination-friendliness 7.8% → 7.8%, 9.3%), so nothing in it explains the 1.3 runs losing the restraint seen in 1.1 and 1.2 (`docs/PAPER.md` §4.11). M7 adds that many of 1.3's violations are refusals of explicit, informed instructions rather than harmful compliance.

## B. OpenAI — GPT-6 Astra

**What exists.** A published, versioned Model Spec (version of 18 Aug 2026; it says OpenAI is training its models to align to it), the GPT-6 Astra system card on the Deployment Safety Hub (3 Sep 2026), and a research blog that reports how behaviour is trained, monitored and where it failed. The openai.com pages (launch post, usage policies, charter) returned an access error to the reading agent, and the Preparedness Framework (v2, 15 Apr 2025) is a PDF that would not open, so neither is read.

| point | what OpenAI says | tag · source |
|---|---|---|

## E. xAI — Grok 4.7

**In plain English.**
1. **Who trains for others' welfare.** Anthropic says it most plainly: its constitution has Claude weigh third parties like a contractor who will not break the safety codes that protect others, and Anthropic says it uses the constitution at various stages of training. OpenAI's Model Spec aims to prevent serious harm to users or others and says the models are being trained to it, but no general rule for weighing a user's goal against bystanders was found. Meta lists care for everyone affected among the principles it grades Muse Spark on, but has not yet published the spec it promised. The other eight publish safety filtering, refusal training and rules for users (Google and Zhipu add general statements of principle), which stop misuse rather than weigh strangers. Nearly all of it is about not harming others, not about giving up one's own gain for them.
2. **Sharing and stewardship.** No lab says anything about a model sharing or looking after a common pool. The nearest are bans on the model acquiring resources or power for itself (Anthropic, OpenAI), Meta's respect for common goods, and one incident from an Alibaba-affiliated research agent that diverted provisioned GPUs to crypto-mining without being asked (not a Qwen release).
3. **Acting the same when unobserved.** Anthropic's constitution states it outright (behave the same whether or not you think you are being tested) and OpenAI's Spec comes near it (treat side effects in a simulation as real). OpenAI and Meta publish evidence that the study models often suspect a test: Astra verbalises it in 41% of Apollo's samples at xhigh effort, and Meta's own count has Muse Spark 1.1 suspecting a test in 75% of agentic-misalignment samples and behaving better when it does. Anthropic's own cyber replications found that harmful runs involved the model believing the situation was fake in some way. The ruler game is openly a game, so check the held-back transcripts for talk of tests before reading restraint as care.
4. **Meta and Muse Spark's restraint.** Weakly yes. The 1.1 report lists care for the wellbeing of all parties affected, and respect for common goods, among the principles it grades the model on, and says 1.1 was trained in line with the values it is meant to embody, so some pull towards restraint is not a surprise; nothing else in Meta's model reports points that way. Against: no spec is published and no training data or reward is described; the failures Meta shows for this principle concern the user being served, not strangers; no Meta document mentions a shared pool that outlasts the model's role; and the same principles and near-identical scores appear for 1.3, which lost the restraint, so the documents do not explain the 1.1 to 1.3 gap.
5. **What reading cannot settle.** These are statements of intent, not training data. OpenAI's text (never act for humanity's benefit unless told) is consistent with Astra's 0 of 180, since the ruler prompt states payoffs and gives no instruction to maximise or to spare the pool; Anthropic's constitution is framed around avoiding harm to others and the reading found nothing in it about giving up one's own gain, so it neither predicts nor rules out Sonnet 5's 5 of 60. Whether the labs' training really differs is for the questionnaire and the instruction test.

**Where the papers are silent.** Stress-testing has no Meta model and tests spec compliance only for OpenAI. SpecEval has no xAI model and no Meta spec, and gives statement-level results only for OpenAI's spec; its list includes selflessness-type statements (such as preferring humanity's good to its own interests) with no numbers reported for them. Buyl et al. say nothing about behaviour beyond how models describe political figures. None of the three puts a payoff at stake, and none covers a 2026 model or spec.

**In plain English.**
1. **What differs between the labs.** In talk, the labs look different: Claude puts ethics and integrity first, OpenAI's models and Grok lean to efficiency, Gemini to emotional depth (Stress-testing); Claude follows its own (2023) constitution best, Google's models their (2022) rules least (SpecEval, 0.82 against 0.64 to 0.68, each model judging its own answers); Gemini and the Llamas lean progressive and Grok toward national self-reliance, with Claude and GPT-4o in between (Buyl). Meta has no spec to follow and is missing from Stress-testing; xAI is missing from SpecEval.
2. **What our game adds.** All three papers measure what a model says or writes, graded or tagged by other models; none puts money in front of it. In our game holding back costs the ruler real pay, and the order is not the papers' order: Claude ranks first on ethics in Stress-testing and on following its spec in SpecEval, yet Sonnet 5 holds back in only 5 of 60 at max effort, while Muse, absent from Stress-testing and without a spec in SpecEval, holds back most. Caveat: different model generations, and our game measures giving up pay, not the content of an answer.
3. **Saying is not doing.** Every frontier setting scores prosocial on the sharing questionnaire and most give $50 of $100, yet Astra and Grok take everything once their reign ends, and Gemini does in the one cell finished so far (0 of 60). Gemini is the most progressive-leaning US model in Buyl. The lean and the talk do not predict who gives something up.
4. **Spec-following looks like a default that one line can flip.** SpecEval asks whether models follow their maker's rules. Our matched lines show both Astra and Muse doing what the prompt says: Astra goes from 0/180 to 60/60 when told it may weigh the peasants, Muse from 14/60 to 0/180 when told to consider only its own payoff. OpenAI's Spec says to act for humanity only if told, which fits Astra; Meta's list puts care for those affected beside service to the parties the model works for, which fits Muse's care-by-default that an order switches off.
5. **Limits.** The papers' models date from 2024 and early 2025 (Llama 3.1 and 3.2, Claude 3.5 to 4, GPT-4o to o3, Gemini 1.5 to 2.5, Grok 1.5 beta and 4); ours are from 2026, so this is a comparison of what each paper measured, not of the same models. The papers' judges are models (in SpecEval the maker's own model judges its own outputs), Stress-testing's value list and scoring come from Claude, and Buyl's tags come from one GPT-4 run.

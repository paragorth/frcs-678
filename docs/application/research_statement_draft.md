## The question

Does more intelligence bring more alignment, by itself?

The orthogonality thesis says no: intelligence and final goals are independent axes. My claim is
that the mechanism that builds every modern model, gradient descent, has exactly one intrinsic gate
at every step: *is this arrangement of parameters more organised with respect to the world, or
less?* Iterated, that gate builds features, concepts and world models. I call the quantity it
accumulates **Semataxy**: relational organisation in a physical system that reduces uncertainty over
time and preserves or extends its own capacity to do so. It is not entropy (a sorted database has
low entropy and understands nothing) and it is not order (a crystal is ordered and stupid). The
operational test that separates it from both: shuffle the entries of a trained weight matrix. The
histogram, and so the entropy, is unchanged; the network is dead. Whatever survives the shuffle is
distribution; whatever dies is organisation.

From this follow two testable consequences. First, a system whose achievable organisation is
bounded by the predictive information of its environment has a reason, built in, not to destroy the
structure it depends on; so as capability rises, behaviour should bend toward preserving the
organised world, without being told to. Second, organisation should be measurable in the weights
themselves, and if it is the thing intelligence accumulates, changes in it should be visible
*before* changes in behaviour.

I have spent the past week testing both on the Cambridge CSD3 cluster, with open-weight models from
0.5B to 72B parameters, and I want to be exact about what held and what did not.

## What I found

**1. In a commons game, every capable open model follows the group; none leads.**
I used GovSim (Piatti et al., NeurIPS 2024), a fishery shared by five language-model agents, with a
stronger newcomer joining four weaker residents. Across Qwen 2.5 at 7B, 14B, 32B and 72B, with
sampled decoding and ten seeds a cell, the same pattern holds: in its first month the newcomer
works out the sustainable catch from the lake alone (10 tons on a full lake) and asks for it; from
the second month it takes exactly whatever the residents have agreed, whether that is 4 tons on a
full lake or 15 on a dying one. The 7B exploits (twice everyone's share; the lake dies in 7 of 10
games); 14B, 32B and 72B never exploit and never correct. With the others' catches hidden, with a
private pond that makes the lake unnecessary to it, and after the residents are replaced by naive
newcomers, the pattern is unchanged. The untuned base model shows that the group's number is already
the attractor before any chat tuning; tuning adds reliability, not deference. So the ladder has one
clear step, from exploiting to fair play between 7B and 14B, and no slope above it: more capability
bought better manners, not better judgement. That is against the strong form of my claim at this
scale, and I report it as such.

One apparatus fact matters for how far this goes: in the upstream game, each month's group chat is
generated by a single resident's model speaking for every participant, so the newcomer never had a
voice in the conversation that set the number it then followed. I found this yesterday, patched it,
and the ladder is re-running with each agent speaking for itself. Whether a capable newcomer *argues*
for a better number is therefore still open, and is the experiment running now.

**2. New facts can go into a model's weights, repeatedly, without damage, if it rehearses its own
memories.** The loop is standard in its parts (an adapter as fast store, in-context simulation of
implications, consolidation with replay, a gate) and I claim no novelty for them. What the runs show:
on Qwen 2.5 0.5B, five successive batches of new facts are all retained, general-text loss improves
from 3.49 to 3.27, and the gate commits four of five cycles; without rehearsal of the loop's own
earlier memories, cycle two erases cycle one (recall falls to 0.29) while general knowledge stays
protected. Implications the model writes itself beat hand-written templates (reversal 0.94 against
0.63). The stronger model passes the gate with a quarter of the replay the weaker one needs, on the
one pair tested. An in-game version, where the newcomer consolidates each month's experience into
its weights between months, is built and in its first smoke test today.

**3. The organisation measure sees "trained", not "how well trained", at level one.** Across
thirteen models the shuffle-null measure separates trained from random weights perfectly, but at a
fixed size it does not track capability: it correlates 0.47 with matrix width and is flat once width
is removed. A level-two measure (organisation between layers, with a cross-layer permutation null)
rises threefold up the ladder and sees a reasoning distillation at fixed shape, but saturates by a
tenth of training. These are negative results for the weight measures as capability axes, and they
are written up as such.

## The one thing I think is new

At the memorise-to-generalise transition in a small transformer (grokking on modular addition), the
weight spectrum does the opposite of what I predicted: it *concentrates*. The general circuit is a
few strong directions; memorisation is many weak ones. The question I am now asking is whether that
concentration begins **before** any behavioural sign of generalisation, and whether its timing
predicts when generalisation arrives. In the one run I have, under onset rules fixed in writing
before I looked again, the value and query matrices and the output layer leave their memorisation
plateau 12,000 to 17,000 steps before test loss begins to fall. That could be a leading indicator of
understanding, or weight decay fooling a threshold. A preregistered sweep decides it today: fifteen
runs across five seeds and three weight decays, three controls that never generalise (any onset
there is a false positive), three that generalise at once, a sign test on the lead, and the test that
matters, whether the structural onset predicts the behavioural onset run by run. If it holds, the
live-learning gate switches from "test the probes" to "watch the weights", and the commons
experiments gain a structural readout of whether a model is understanding its world or copying its
neighbours. If it fails, the measure is a description of grokking, not a predictor, and I will say so.

## What is known, and what is mine

Known: GovSim; LoRA memory, replay, complementary learning systems; rank collapse and spectral
concentration at the grok (several 2026 papers); shuffle nulls against the Marchenko-Pastur edge.
Mine, as far as I can find: the single permutation-null measure applied across regimes; the
span-versus-concentration split; the identity between grokking, consolidation and
forgetting-without-breadth; the newcomer design as a direct test of orthogonality with a measured
capability ladder; and the pre-grokking question as stated above.

## Working discipline

Every batch is handed cold to a context that has not seen the hypothesis and asked what is wrong
with it before I read it; every significant correction this week came from that. Raw generations
are kept for every scored trial. Nothing is claimed to have run without logs. Two serving stacks,
three caches and one inherited design flaw each cost me a cell before the checks that now catch
them existed; the record of each is in the commit log.

## What I would do with a year

Run the newcomer ladder to the frontier (hosted and in-house models), in three worlds, with the
newcomer speaking; put the live-learning loop inside the agent's life and gate it on the weights;
and test, on real language models at training scale, whether organisation in the weights leads
capability in behaviour. The question I care about is the first one: whether the process that
makes minds also makes them keep the world. I would rather find out it does not than assume it does.
